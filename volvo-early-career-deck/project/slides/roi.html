Core message: we will prove the return with data, not assert it. Talking track: The table shows the six metrics I propose we report to this team. Time to independent contribution, because a graduate who is productive in months rather than a year is the most direct return. Retention at 24 months, because the most expensive hire is the one who leaves after we have trained them. Quality of hire, measured as the manager’s rating at twelve months. The share of hires deployed on flagship projects, where the target is 100 percent by design. Offer acceptance at our partner campuses, which tells us whether we are winning against tech companies. And, longer term, the share of the cohort in our leadership pipeline by year five. I have left the baselines and targets blank on purpose: HR analytics will pull today’s numbers from our systems before the pilot starts, and I would rather commit to targets we can defend. On the right, the economics. The investment is mainly assessor time, events and an assessment platform. The return is faster productivity, fewer mis-hires and a deeper bench. The main risks, inconsistent assessors, bias and a heavy candidate experience, have named controls. And the legacy-hired control group means we will know, not guess, whether this works.
